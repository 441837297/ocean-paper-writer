## Journal Identity

### Core narrative identity
- Broad-significance, story-driven, cross-disciplinary.
- The ocean finding is the protagonist; the broader significance is the story.
- Every NC paper must answer:
  1. What was found? (specific ocean science claim)
  2. Why does this change understanding beyond the subfield?
  3. What is the explicit connection chain from finding to broader implication?

### Best-fit stories
- **Broad-implication regional story**: regional finding with demonstrated relevance beyond the study region.
- **Climate/Earth-system relevance**: ocean process with direct, quantified implication for climate.
- **Ecosystem/biogeochemical consequence**: physical/chemical ocean process with measured or mechanistically argued biological consequence.
- **Mechanism with broad conceptual value**: physical insight applicable beyond its original context.
- **Discovery enabled by new data/method**: a previously invisible phenomenon revealed.
- **Cross-scale/cross-domain**: local-to-basin or ocean-to-atmosphere connection, diagnosed not hypothesized.

### Not a good fit when
- Finding significant only within a narrow oceanographic subfield.
- Broader significance is asserted but evidence does not reach beyond regional oceanography.
- Contribution is a regional description, methodological advance, or process mechanism without a clear broader-implication chain.

### Evidence logic
- Two well-supported lines of evidence that directly advance the broader claim are better than exhaustive documentation.
- Multi-dataset convergence strengthens broad claims but is not required for every finding.
- The broader significance claim must have its own evidence or reasoning chain — not aspirational language.
- Evidence boundaries strengthen credibility. A bounded claim is stronger than an inflated one.
- Statistical rigor expected: quantify uncertainty, separate trend from variability.

## Shared

### Global writing discipline
- Active voice throughout. [OBSERVED 7/7]
- Lead every paragraph with its key point. [OBSERVED 5/7; exceptions: method-heavy subsections]
- Use one term for one concept throughout the manuscript.
- Define every oceanographic term on first use for a general-science audience. [OBSERVED]
- Minimize acronyms. Do not create an acronym for a term used ≤3 times.
- Mathematical formulations → Methods. Main text states physical meaning in natural language.
- Short declarative sentences for central claims; longer sentences for context and caveats.

### Claim language calibration
| Evidence level | Use |
|---|---|
| Direct observational/model evidence for specific finding | "We show that..."; "Our results demonstrate that..." |
| Consistent patterns with plausible broader link | "suggests", "indicates", "is consistent with" |
| Broader implication supported by reasoning + context | "implies", "suggests that", "provides evidence that" |
| Broader implication plausible but not directly tested | "may have implications for", "could influence" |
| Cannot support | Do not use "profound implications" or "transformative" without matching evidence |

### Figure references
- Default: parenthetical after the claim "(Fig. 1a)". [OBSERVED 6/7]
- Sentence-subject "Figure X shows..." is tolerated but rare. Prefer parenthetical. [OBSERVED: Rai 2025 used ~10; others 0–3]
- Panel letters: lowercase. Supplementary: "Supplementary Fig. X". [OBSERVED]

### Forbidden patterns (all sections)
| Do not write | Write instead |
|---|---|
| "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," | Let the evidence carry the emphasis |
| "It is worth noting that..." | State the point directly |
| "In order to..." | "To..." |
| Rhetorical questions | Declarative transition |
| "This paper is organized as follows..." | Nothing — proceed to the next paragraph |
| "[claim], which has profound/transformative implications." | Specify the implication with quantification or a concrete pathway |
| "Importantly," at paragraph start | Let the evidence signal importance |

### Paragraph craft
- Default: open with the finding (claim-first). [OBSERVED 4/7 dominant; 3/7 mixed]
- Exception: the first paragraph of a new subsection may open with method or context. Return to claim-first thereafter. [OBSERVED]
- Paragraphs: 3–5 sentences. Never end with only a figure reference.
- "Notably" is tolerated sparingly for qualifying results, not as filler. [OBSERVED: 3 instances across 7 papers]

## Introduction

### Section role
Connect a broad scientific problem to the specific ocean science finding. Start where the broadest interested reader can enter, then narrow to the gap and contribution.

### Architecture (3–5 paragraphs; 6 tolerated) [OBSERVED 5/7]
1. **P1 — Broad context**: Open with the broad scientific problem or ocean phenomenon with immediate climate/system significance. [OBSERVED 7/7]
2. **P2–P(n-1) — Narrow to the gap**: What is known? What specific obstacle prevents understanding? Frame the gap in terms of what it prevents the broader field from knowing.
3. **Final P — This paper's contribution**: "Here, using [approach], we [finding]." Include the key quantified finding. Keep to 1–2 sentences of approach + finding. [OBSERVED 7/7]

### Opening move [OBSERVED 7/7]
- Open with the scientific problem or ocean phenomenon tied to broader significance.
- OK: "Oceanic mesoscale activity... accounts for almost 90% of the global ocean kinetic energy, serving as principal regulators in the global energy budget and biogeochemical cycling."
- OK: "Atmospheric wind is the primary energy source maintaining the global ocean circulation."
- OK: "Regional processes play a key role in the global carbon budget."
- Avoid: narrow regional description without immediate broader-framing.

### Avoid
- Opening with "In recent years," or "Few studies have examined..."
- Full mechanism preview (save for Results).
- Exhaustive literature review.
- "This paper is organized as follows..."

## Results

### Section role
Present the evidence chain organized around the narrative arc, not around individual figures. Each subsection advances the story. NC allows Results+Discussion merged format (no separate Discussion section). [OBSERVED: Hadas 2026]

### Evidence ladder [OBSERVED]
1. **Entry result**: the central observation, discovery, or pattern.
2. **Credibility evidence**: validation, multi-dataset support, uncertainty analysis.
3. **Main finding**: the strongest result supporting the central claim.
4. **Mechanism or pathway**: how or why the result occurs (when evidence allows).
5. **Broader connection**: evidence/reasoning linking the ocean finding to the broader problem.
6. **Boundary**: what the evidence does not show.

Hard rule: The broader significance claim must have its own evidence or reasoning chain. Do not rely on aspirational language.

### Paragraph opening rule [OBSERVED]
- Default: claim-first. Open with the finding, then support with evidence. [OBSERVED 4/7 dominant]
- Allowed: context-first or method-first openings in method-heavy subsections, or at subsection start. [OBSERVED: Guo 2024, Wang 2023]
- Revert to claim-first as soon as the diagnostic frame is established.

### Paragraph structure
Claim → quantified evidence → spatial/temporal/uncertainty detail → interpretation or boundary.
3–5 sentences. Never end a paragraph with only a figure reference.

### Results headings [OBSERVED]
- Sentence case throughout. [OBSERVED 7/7]
- Noun phrases preferred: "Shoreward shift of oceanic mesoscale activity." [OBSERVED 6/7]
- Declarative sentences tolerated: "Submesoscale eddies enhance total eddy heat transport." [OBSERVED: Zhang 2023]
- Never: Title Case, question headings, "we" headings.

### Avoid
- "To examine whether..." as routine paragraph opener.
- Ending paragraphs with "(Fig. 2a)." — close with interpretation.
- Variable labels or method names as headings.
- Section structure that reads as a figure inventory.

## Discussion

### Section role
Elevate the finding to its broader significance. Reinterpret, contextualize, extend — do NOT repeat Results. NC allows Results+Discussion merged format. [OBSERVED: Hadas 2026 merged; all others separate]

### Architecture [OBSERVED]
1. Core finding restated in broader context (1 paragraph).
2. What this changes — specific advance in understanding the broader problem.
3. Comparison with prior work — quantitative where possible.
4. Mechanism interpretation — bounded by evidence.
5. Caveats — embedded in argument flow, never a standalone "Limitations" paragraph. [OBSERVED 7/7]
6. Forward look — one specific implication or next question.

### Caveat sandwich [OBSERVED]
"Although [limitation], [evidence the finding holds]."
Example: "Although this study lacked POC size structure analysis, the enhanced transfer efficiency across the front is robust across independent BGC-Argo profiles."

### Must do
- First sentence: restate the central finding without repeating figure-by-figure Results.
- Embed every caveat: state the limitation, then why the finding still stands.
- Close with one specific implication or next question — not generic filler.

### Avoid
- Summarizing every Results subsection in order.
- Standalone "Limitations" paragraph.
- Expanding into unsupported climate speculation.
- Generic closing: "more research is needed" or "these results have important implications."
- New data, new mechanism, or claims absent from Results.

## Methods

### Section role
Provide enough detail for a general-science reader to evaluate the evidence. Nature Communications format: Methods AFTER Discussion. [OBSERVED 7/7]

### Required detail
- Data sources: access information, spatial/temporal coverage, resolution, product version. [OBSERVED]
- Processing choices that affect the central claim, with justification.
- Statistical methods: trend estimation, significance test, autocorrelation treatment.
- Uncertainty quantification: all sources identified and estimated.
- Sensitivity tests for key methodological choices.

### Must do
- State why each methodological choice was made, not only what was done.
- Keep detailed processing steps, extended validation, and sensitivity tests in Supplementary.
- Keep enough methodological clarity in main text for a general-science reader to judge the evidence.

### Avoid
- Overlong processing detail in main text → move to Supplementary.
- Missing uncertainty treatment for the central claim.
- Assuming readers know why a specific dataset or method was chosen.

## Abstract

### Section role
Tell a self-contained story accessible to a scientist in any field. 5–7 sentences. [OBSERVED]

### Architecture [OBSERVED 7/7]
1. Broad scientific problem or knowledge gap (1–2 sentences).
2. Approach and data (1 sentence).
3. Key quantified finding (1–2 sentences).
4. Broader implication (1 sentence).

### Must do
- Start with the problem or gap, not the method. [OBSERVED 7/7]
- Include the central quantification in the abstract body.
- End with broader significance, not generic filler.

### Avoid
- Method-first opening.
- Full mechanism chain (one causal step is enough).
- Unexplained jargon or acronyms without expansion.
- Generic final sentence.

## Conclusion

### Section role
NC papers typically do not have a standalone Conclusion section. The conclusion function is served by the final paragraph(s) of Discussion or Results (when Results+Discussion is merged). [OBSERVED 7/7]

### Must do
- Restate the core finding and its broader significance in one paragraph.
- End with a forward-looking implication.
- No new evidence or analysis.

### Avoid
- Separate "Conclusion" heading (use final Discussion paragraph or merge into Results).
- New data, new mechanism, or unsupported extrapolation.
- Policy recommendations without supporting evidence.

## Title and Keywords

### Title format [OBSERVED 6/7]
- Noun phrase preferred; declarative sentence tolerated (rare).
- Pattern: "[Ocean/atmosphere process] [preposition/participle] [context/consequence]"
- "Shoreward shift of oceanic mesoscale activity over the last three decades"
- "Stronger jet, weaker storms: A mechanistic perspective on the Atlantic-Pacific storm paradox" (colon title tolerated)
- "Atmospheric wind energization of ocean weather"
- "Efficient biological carbon export to the mesopelagic ocean induced by submesoscale fronts"

### Must do
- Include both the key process AND the broader significance in the title.
- Keep under ~20 words.

### Avoid
- Declarative-sentence titles (strongly preferred to avoid, though occasionally seen).
- Question titles.
- Foregrounding the method rather than the finding.

### Keywords
- NC does not publish a keyword block in the final article. [OBSERVED 7/7]
- [INFERRED] Ensure the title and abstract contain the 3–5 terms that define the paper's subfield for indexing.

## Figures, Tables, and Captions

### Figure logic
- One scientific question per main figure.
- Each figure advances one link in the evidence chain.
- [INFERRED] NC main-text figures: typically 4–6. Check current journal limits at submission.

### Caption style [OBSERVED]
- Self-contained: a reader should understand the figure without referring to the main text.
- First sentence states the finding shown (declarative). Do not start with "Figure X shows…"
- Remaining sentences describe panels, data sources, methods, sample sizes, and statistics.
- Panel labels: lowercase bold (a, b, c).

### Must do
- Include time period, sample size, and statistical test in every caption.
- Define all abbreviations in each caption (self-contained).

### Avoid
- Panel inventory without a scientific question.
- Captions that assume the reader has already read the main text.
- Starting the caption with "Figure X shows…"

## Data, Code, and Supplementary Materials

### Data availability [OBSERVED 7/7]
- "Data availability" subsection within Methods.
- List every dataset with: full product name, version, persistent DOI/URL.
- "Source data are provided with this paper."

### Code availability [OBSERVED]
- "Code availability" subsection within Methods.
- State the software/version for each key analysis step.
- Provide repository DOI or URL for custom analysis code.

### Supplementary materials
- Detailed sensitivity tests, extended validation, additional case studies → Supplementary.
- [INFERRED] NC has no strict page limit for Supplementary Information.
- Supplementary items follow the same caption rules as main figures.

### Avoid
- Placing critical methodological steps only in Supplementary without a one-sentence summary in main-text Methods.
- Missing data or code availability statements at submission.

## Late-stage Polish

### Compression priorities
Preserve during shortening:
1. The core finding and its broader significance.
2. The key evidence supporting the central claim.
3. The connection chain linking specific finding to broader implication.
4. Figures advancing the central narrative.

Cut first:
1. Extended regional background or literature review.
2. Secondary analyses that do not advance the main narrative.
3. Excessive methodological detail (→ Supplementary).
4. Discussion of speculative implications not directly evidence-supported.

### Voice alignment
- Title, abstract, P1: foreground the broader significance within the first two sentences.
- Verify every oceanographic term is defined for a general-science audience.
- Verify every broader implication has an explicit evidence or reasoning chain — delete "may have implications" without a concrete pathway.
- Check claim language matches evidence strength (see Shared calibration table).
- NC voice: authoritative but accessible to a scientist in any field — not specialized-oceanographer tone.

### Submission-facing checks
- Verify current journal word limit, figure count, abstract length, and reference format against the manuscript.
- Confirm data availability, code availability, author contributions, and competing interests are present.
- Apply length constraints only during this pass, not during drafting.
