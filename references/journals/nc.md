# Nature Communications

*Nature Communications*

---

## 1. Journal Narrative Identity

Nature Communications is a **broad-significance, story-driven, cross-disciplinary science journal**.
It does not publish narrow regional studies, descriptive data reports, or incremental advances unless they are connected to a broader scientific question.
It publishes research where the finding carries implications beyond the immediate subfield and is presented in a narrative accessible to a scientifically literate general-science audience.

In ocean science, Nature Communications is the right home when you have:

- A finding that changes understanding of a broader scientific problem — climate processes, ecosystem function, biogeochemical cycles, Earth system dynamics, or global change — not just an oceanographic subfield
- A story that can be told without requiring specialist jargon to convey its significance
- Evidence strong enough to support the broader claim, with the evidence chain presented clearly

Nature Communications is **not** the right home when:

- The finding is significant only within a narrow oceanographic subfield and cannot be connected to a broader scientific question
- The broader significance is stated but the evidence does not directly support it — the paper would need to add a new data source, analysis, or coupling step to make the connection credible
- The contribution is a regional description, a methodological advance, or a process-level mechanism without a clear implication for how it changes broader understanding

The core narrative personality of Nature Communications is: **significant, accessible, story-shaped**.
A successful NC paper makes readers from other scientific fields understand why the finding matters, without requiring them to become oceanographers.
The contribution is measured by how much it shifts understanding of a broader problem, not by the completeness of the local evidence chain.

## 2. Best-fit Ocean Science Stories

### Broad-implication regional story

A regional oceanographic study that reveals or constrains a process with relevance beyond the study region — for example, how a specific coastal upwelling regime responds to climate forcing in a way that informs understanding of eastern boundary upwelling systems globally.
The regional-to-general connection must be explicit and evidence-supported, not asserted.

### Climate or Earth-system relevance story

A study where the ocean finding has direct implications for climate variability, climate change, sea-level rise, ocean heat content, carbon uptake, cryosphere-ocean interaction, or hydrological cycle.
The connection between the ocean process and the climate-scale implication should be explicit and quantified where possible.

### Ecosystem or biogeochemical consequence story

A finding about a physical or chemical ocean process with measured or mechanistically argued consequences for marine ecosystems, biogeochemical cycles, oxygen, acidification, primary productivity, or carbon export.
The ecological or biogeochemical link should be demonstrated, not merely proposed.

### Mechanism with broad conceptual value

A mechanism study where the physical insight has implications beyond its original context — for example, an instability mechanism that explains eddy generation in multiple regions, or a mixing parameterization that improves climate models globally.
The conceptual generalizability should be argued with evidence, not assumed.

### Discovery enabled by new data or method

A new observational dataset, a novel application of remote sensing or AI, a data-assimilative product, or a sensor advancement that reveals a phenomenon or pattern previously invisible.
The discovery itself is the story, and the method is the enabler.

### Cross-scale or cross-domain story

A study that connects local-scale ocean processes to basin-scale or global-scale phenomena — for example, how coastal buoyancy forcing influences large-scale overturning, or how tropical instability waves affect atmospheric deep convection.
The cross-scale connection should be diagnosed, not merely hypothesized.

### Stories that belong elsewhere:

| Better fit for | Story characteristics |
|---------------|----------------------|
| GRL | Single sharp finding, limited but sufficient evidence, significance within ocean science |
| JGR-Oceans | Complete multi-evidence chain, regional description, validation-focused, ocean science audience |
| JPO | Physical mechanism diagnosis, process-oriented analysis, specialist physical oceanography audience |
| Nature Climate Change | Climate change detection and attribution, policy-relevant, focused on climate system rather than ocean process per se |
| Scientific Reports | Sound but incremental science, regional without broader connection, method-focused without discovery |

## 3. Preferred Reasoning and Evidence Logic

### Claim structure

NC claims operate at two levels.
The **specific ocean science claim** (what was observed, what mechanism was diagnosed) must be well-supported.
The **broader significance claim** (why this matters beyond the subfield) must follow from the specific claim through an explicit connection chain, not through aspirational language.

| Claim level | What supports it |
|-------------|-----------------|
| Specific finding | Direct evidence: observations, model output, diagnostics, statistics |
| Broader implication | Connection chain: why this finding changes understanding of a larger problem, supported by reasoning, context, or additional evidence |

### How much evidence is enough

NC requires **enough evidence to convince a skeptical general-science reader that both the specific finding and its broader significance are credible**.
Key principles:

- **Selective but convincing evidence:** It is better to show two well-supported lines of evidence that directly advance the broader claim than to exhaustively document every aspect of the study.
NC readers value narrative clarity over completeness.
- **The broader claim must have its own evidence support, not just language.** Saying "these results have implications for global carbon cycle projections" requires either a direct demonstration (e.g., the mechanism changes a model's carbon uptake) or a well-reasoned chain (e.g., the process operates at a scale that current models miss, and the magnitude implies a non-trivial flux).
- **Evidence boundaries are part of the story.** Acknowledging where the evidence cannot reach strengthens credibility.
A paper that says "this mechanism may contribute to the observed trend, but decadal variability in the record makes a definitive attribution premature" is more convincing than one that overclaims.
- **Convergence across independent approaches strengthens broad claims.** The broader the claim, the more important it is to show that multiple lines of evidence (observations, models, theory, proxies) point in the same direction.

### Mechanism language

| Evidence base | Language |
|---------------|----------|
| Direct observational or model evidence for the specific finding | "shows", "reveals", "demonstrates" |
| Consistent patterns with plausible broader link | "suggests", "indicates", "is consistent with" |
| Broader implication supported by reasoning and context | "implies", "suggests that", "provides evidence that" |
| Broader implication plausible but not directly tested | "may have implications for", "could influence", "highlights the need to" |

### Ocean science-specific evidence logic for NC

- **From local to broad: show the connection chain.** A regional finding about Southern Ocean mixed-layer deepening is not automatically a global carbon cycle story.
The paper must demonstrate (or at minimum argue with quantitative context) how the local process scales, connects, or informs the broader problem.
- **Multi-dataset consistency as credibility.** For broad-significance claims, showing that multiple independent data sources (satellite, in situ, reanalysis, model) support the same conclusion significantly strengthens the paper.
- **Climate or ecosystem connection: be explicit.** Saying "this matters for climate" is weak.
Saying "this process accounts for X% of the interannual variability in basin-scale heat uptake" is strong.
- **Temporal context for significance.** An observed trend or event should be placed in the context of decadal variability, the observational record length, and model projections.
This is particularly important when the broader significance depends on whether the finding represents a persistent change or a transient fluctuation.
- **Uncertainty as a guide, not a weakness.** NC allows uncertainty to be part of the narrative — acknowledging what is not known can strengthen the case for why more research is needed and why the finding changes the framing.

### Uncertainty handling

- State uncertainties quantitatively where possible (confidence intervals, ensemble spread, sensitivity range).
- When uncertainty is large, acknowledge it explicitly and explain what additional evidence would reduce it.
- Do not hide uncertainty behind hedging language ("may potentially possibly" stacks).
One clear bounded statement is better than six layers of hedge.
- If the broader implication depends on an uncertain projection or extrapolation, make that dependency clear.

## 4. Section-level Story Strategy

### Introduction

NC Introduction should **connect a broad scientific problem to the specific ocean science question**.
It should start where the broadest interested reader can enter, then narrow to the gap and the contribution.

A strong NC Introduction arc:
(1) open with the broad scientific problem — climate feedback, carbon cycle, ecosystem services, sea-level rise, extreme events;
(2) narrow to the specific knowledge gap framed in terms of what it prevents the broader field from understanding;
(3) state what this study does and reveals, with the key finding and its broader significance.

Avoid:
opening with a narrow regional description, beginning with "In recent years," exhaustive literature review, or framing the gap as "few studies have examined."

### Methods

NC Methods should be **transparent enough for evaluation but structured so the main text narrative is not interrupted**.
Methods can appear after the main text or in supplementary material.

Essential elements:
data sources with access information;
key processing steps that affect interpretation;
statistical or analytical framework;
uncertainty quantification relevant to main claims;
and model configuration or experimental design if applicable.

Technical details (extended validation, parameter sensitivity, additional case studies) can go to supplementary material.
The main text should retain enough methodological clarity for a general-science reader to judge whether the evidence supports the claims.

### Results

NC Results should be organized around the **narrative arc, not around individual figures or analyses**.
Each subsection should advance the story: set up what question is being addressed, present the key evidence, and connect to the next step.

A typical structure:
(1) core observation or finding;
(2) supporting evidence;
(3) mechanism or pathway;
(4) broader context — how the finding connects to the larger scientific problem.

Key principles:
- Lead each subsection with the finding, then the evidence
- Each figure should serve one specific purpose in the narrative
- Figures should have self-contained captions interpretable without the main text
- Quantitative results should include uncertainties where relevant

### NC Results evidence ladder

For Nature Communications, the generic Results evidence ladder should usually be mapped as:

1. **Entry result** — the central observation, discovery, or pattern that opens the story.
2. **Credibility evidence** — validation, independent dataset support, benchmark, uncertainty, or sensitivity analysis.
3. **Main finding** — the strongest result that supports the central claim.
4. **Mechanism or pathway** — diagnostic evidence showing how or why the result occurs, if supported.
5. **Broader connection** — evidence or reasoning that links the specific ocean finding to the broader scientific problem.
6. **Boundary** — what the evidence does not show, especially for regional-to-global, correlation-to-causation, or climate-relevance claims.

Hard rule:
The broader significance claim must have its own evidence or reasoning chain. Do not rely on aspirational language.

### Discussion

NC Discussion should **elevate the finding to its broader significance while remaining evidence-bounded**:
(1) restate the core finding briefly;
(2) what this changes — be specific about how the result advances understanding of the broader problem;
(3) compare with previous understanding;
(4) bound the claim — under what conditions does this apply, what remains uncertain;
(5) forward look — a question it opens, a prediction it enables, or an approach it validates.

NC Discussion should not repeat Results in detail, expand into unsupported speculation, or end with "need for further research."

### Conclusion

NC Conclusion can be brief — a single paragraph that restates the core finding, its broader significance, and one forward-looking implication.
It should not introduce new evidence or analysis.
The Conclusion should leave the general-science reader with a clear answer to: "Why does this matter?"

### Abstract

NC Abstract should tell a **self-contained story**: broad problem → knowledge gap → approach → key finding → broader implication.
Start with the broad scientific problem, not the oceanographic subfield.
End with the broader implication.
The Abstract should be accessible to a scientist in any field: no unexplained jargon, acronyms without expansion, or excessive quantification.
A strong NC Abstract makes a non-oceanographer want to read the paper.

## 5. Writing Voice and Rhetorical Style

### Sentence and paragraph rhythm

NC writing is **clear and direct, with a narrative drive**.
Sentences are shorter and more varied than in JGR-Oceans or JPO.
Paragraphs are compact and each advances the story.
The writing should feel purposeful, not dense.

- Active voice throughout: "We show that" not "It is shown that."
- Avoid filler transitions:
"Importantly", "Notably", "Interestingly" — cut these unless they signal genuine significance.
- Open paragraphs with the key point, then support with evidence.

### Claim strength and hedging

NC claims should be **confident within their bounds**:

- When the specific finding is robust: "We show that", "Our results demonstrate that"
- When the broader implication follows from the evidence: "These results suggest that", "This implies"
- When the connection is plausible but not demonstrated: "This may have implications for", "These findings highlight"

Inflated claims harm credibility.
A bounded claim with clear evidence is more effective for NC's broad audience than a grand claim that invites skepticism.
NC readers include researchers from other fields who may not know the oceanographic context — the connection chain between evidence and the broader claim must be explicit, not rhetorical.

### Level of technicality

NC is read by a **broad scientific audience**, not just oceanographers.
Define oceanographic terms on first use, even common ones that are not standard across geoscience.
Minimize acronyms and define on first use (EKE → eddy kinetic energy, MLD → mixed-layer depth).
Avoid equations in the main text unless essential; key equations belong in Methods or supplementary material.
Frame quantitative results in natural language: "the warming rate increased threefold" is more accessible than a dense numerical listing — provide the interpretation first, with numbers as support.

### Broader significance framing

Broader significance must be **evidence-connected, not rhetorical**.
"The observed increase in eddy kinetic energy implies a 15% enhancement in eddy heat transport" is appropriate.
"These results have profound implications for global climate policy" is inflated unless each link in the chain (eddy → heat transport → sea-ice → ecosystem → policy) is defensible.
NC editors and reviewers are trained to detect significance inflation — a well-bounded, specific statement is more convincing than a sweeping one.

### Writing voice for ocean science in NC

The ocean science finding should be the **protagonist of the story**, framed in terms of what it reveals about a broader problem.
The writing should make clear what was found, how it was found (briefly), why it matters beyond the subfield, and what we now understand differently.
The voice should be **authoritative but not specialized** — as if explaining the significance to a colleague in another department.

## 6. Late-stage Compression and Submission Notes

### What to preserve during compression

- The core finding and its broader significance
- The key evidence that supports the central claim
- The connection chain that links the specific finding to the broader implication
- Figures that tell the essential story

### What to cut first if over length

- Extended regional background or literature review
- Secondary analyses that support but do not advance the main narrative
- Excessive methodological detail (move to supplementary material)
- Discussion of implications that are speculative or not directly evidence-supported

### Hard constraints to verify

At the final pre-submission stage, check the journal's current author guidelines for word count, figure count, abstract length, and format requirements.
Apply these only during the late polishing pass, not during drafting.
Verify data availability statement, author contributions, and competing interests declarations.

### Structure fit notes

- **Introduction opens too narrowly:** If the first paragraph starts with a regional current or a specific dataset rather than a broad scientific problem, a general-science reader may not see why the paper matters.
- **Broader significance asserted but not supported:** If the Discussion claims broad implications without a clear connection chain, the paper may be better suited to JGR-Oceans or GRL.
- **Abstract reads like a technical summary:** If the abstract can be rewritten with the same content but without mentioning a broader problem or implication, the narrative is not shaped for NC.
- **Evidence boundaries are missing:** If the paper makes broad claims without acknowledging limitations, the connection between evidence and the broader claim is weakened.
A bounded claim with uncertainty is stronger for NC.
