## Journal Identity

### Core narrative identity
- Complete, rigorous, thorough. A full evidence chain from observation through analysis to physical interpretation.
- Multiple lines of independent evidence are expected, not optional.
- The reader finishes knowing not just what was found, but why it is the correct interpretation and what its limitations are.
- A successful JGR-Oceans paper tells a complete story where each link in the chain is documented and justified.

### Best-fit stories
- **Complete observational-mechanistic study**: detection → characterization → mechanism → implication, all documented.
- **Multi-evidence synthesis**: satellite + in situ + reanalysis + model convergence on a central finding.
- **Regional process study with broader relevance**: detailed enough to inform understanding of analogous systems.
- **Method-enabled scientific study with validation**: new method applied to a scientific question, thoroughly validated.
- **Process-oriented modeling study**: tests a mechanism or quantifies a process, with sensitivity tests and uncertainty assessment.

### Not a good fit when
- Single sharp finding without needing multiple evidence lines — consider GRL.
- Evidence chain incomplete — suggestive pattern without supporting diagnostics.
- Mechanism is entirely speculative without process-oriented analysis.
- Contribution is primarily methodological without a clear scientific finding.

### Evidence logic
- Multiple lines of independent evidence are the gold standard. Satellite alone is weaker than satellite + in situ + diagnostics.
- Validation is not optional: central claims require validation against independent observations in the main text.
- Uncertainty quantification is expected: error bars, confidence intervals, ensemble spread, sensitivity tests.
- Trend claims require explicit demonstration that record length separates trend from natural variability. Multi-decadal records expected.
- Model-observation comparison: model biases must be acknowledged and their impact on conclusions discussed.
- Seasonal cycle treatment: must demonstrate proper separation of seasonal, interannual, and trend signals.

## Shared

### Global writing discipline
- Active voice throughout: "We analyzed..." not "An analysis was conducted." [OBSERVED 5/5]
- Lead paragraphs with the finding, then describe supporting evidence. [OBSERVED: mixed, see Results section]
- Use one term for one concept throughout the manuscript.
- Define domain terminology on first use. JGR-Oceans readers are specialist ocean scientists but include non-specialist geoscientists.
- Minimize acronyms. Do not create an acronym for a term used ≤3 times.
- Mathematical equations → Methods or accompanied by plain-language physical explanation in main text.
- Paragraphs: 4–8 sentences, allowing one idea to develop with supporting evidence. [OBSERVED]

### Claim language calibration
| Evidence level | Use |
|---|---|
| Direct quantitative evidence with uncertainty, process diagnostics | "demonstrates", "establishes", "confirms" |
| Strong pattern with plausible mechanism | "indicates", "suggests", "is consistent with" |
| Plausible but not directly tested | "may", "could", "possibly" |
| Cannot support | Do not claim "demonstrates" for correlative evidence |

### Figure references
- Always use full word "Figure" (never "Fig.") — AGU style. [OBSERVED 5/5]
- Parenthetical "(Figure 1a)" is the default. [OBSERVED: dominant in 4/5 papers]
- Sentence-subject "Figure X shows..." is acceptable and used regularly in JGR-Oceans. [OBSERVED: common in Cai 2024, present in all]
- Passive constructions "are shown in Figure X" are also standard. [OBSERVED: dominant in Wang 2023]
- Panel letters: lowercase. Supplementary: "Figure S1".

### Forbidden patterns (all sections)
| Do not write | Write instead |
|---|---|
| "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," | Let the evidence carry the emphasis |
| "It is worth noting that..." | State the point directly |
| "In order to..." | "To..." |
| "Fig." as abbreviation | "Figure" |
| "[claim], which has important implications for climate." | Specify the implication with quantification or a concrete pathway |
| Inflated claims without support | Match claim strength to evidence |

### JGR-Oceans specific requirements
- **Plain Language Summary**: required by AGU. [OBSERVED 5/5]
- **Key Points**: optional but common. 3 bullet points after abstract.
- Methods BEFORE Results (standard IMRaD). [OBSERVED 5/5]
- Discussion and Conclusions typically merged into one section. [OBSERVED 4/5 merge; Wang 2023 had both separate]
- "Conclusions and Discussion" or "Discussion and Conclusions" — order varies. [OBSERVED]

## Introduction

### Section role
Establish the broader context, identify the knowledge gap, and state the study's objective and approach. More developed than in letter-format journals. [OBSERVED]

### Architecture (3–5 paragraphs) [OBSERVED: range 3–5]
1. **P1 — Broad context**: The phenomenon, its importance, and current knowledge (4–6 sentences). [OBSERVED 5/5]
2. **P2 — Knowledge gap**: What is not understood, what evidence conflicts, what methodological limitation has prevented progress.
3. **P3 — This study**: Objective, approach, and how it addresses the gap.
4. **Optional final P — Roadmap**: Brief preview of paper structure. [OBSERVED in some but not all]

### Opening move [OBSERVED 5/5]
- Open with the phenomenon's importance with quantitative anchoring.
- OK: "The Southern Ocean is one of the most eddy-rich regions of the world's ocean."
- OK: "Mesoscale ocean variability with spatial scales of tens to hundreds of kilometers is comprised of features such as vortices, waves, and jets."
- Avoid: "In recent years, the study of X has attracted increasing attention."

### Must do
- Include a comprehensive but not exhaustive literature review covering the most directly relevant studies.
- State the gap precisely — not "few studies have examined" but what specific obstacle prevented understanding.
- End with what this study does and how it addresses the gap.

### Avoid
- Jumping directly to the gap without establishing context (underserves the readership).
- Full mechanism preview (save for Results).
- Generic gap statements ("more research is needed").

## Results

### Section role
Present the evidence chain completely. JGR-Oceans Results can be organized by evidence type or by sub-claim. More comprehensive than GRL. [OBSERVED]

### Evidence ladder [OBSERVED]
1. **Description**: spatial pattern, temporal evolution, basic statistics.
2. **Quantification**: magnitudes, uncertainties, statistical significance.
3. **Mechanism/diagnosis**: process-oriented analysis (energy conversion, budget closure, scaling).
4. **Robustness**: sensitivity tests, cross-dataset validation.

### Paragraph opening rule [OBSERVED]
- Claim-first is preferred but JGR-Oceans allows more variety than other journals. [OBSERVED: claim-first ranges 29–67%]
- Context-first openings are common when introducing new diagnostics or reviewing prior findings. [OBSERVED: dominant in Fu 2023 at 53%]
- Figure-first openings acceptable: "Figure 3 displays the long-term mean..." [OBSERVED: dominant in Cai 2024 at 56%]
- Method-first openings for new diagnostic introductions: "To quantify the contributions...", "The LEC is used to explain..." [OBSERVED: dominant in Wang 2023 at 50%]
- Never open with rhetorical filler.

### Paragraph structure
Claim or context → quantified evidence → spatial/temporal/uncertainty detail → interpretation.
4–8 sentences. JGR-Oceans paragraphs develop one idea with supporting evidence.

### Results headings [OBSERVED]
- Both Title Case and Sentence case observed. Title Case more common. [OBSERVED 3/5 Title Case, 2/5 Sentence case]
- Noun phrases exclusively: "Eddy Energy Budget," "Spatiotemporal Characteristics of EKE."
- Declarative sentence headings: not observed. Avoid.
- Numbered subsections standard: "3.1.", "3.2.", etc.

### Avoid
- Figure-by-figure walkthrough as the only organizing principle.
- Descriptive-only Results without mechanistic interpretation.
- Ending paragraphs with only a figure reference.
- Variable labels as headings.

## Discussion

### Section role
Interpret findings, compare with prior work, and discuss implications. In JGR-Oceans, Discussion is typically merged with Conclusions. [OBSERVED 4/5 papers merge Discussion and Conclusions]

### Architecture [OBSERVED]
1. Summary of main findings (1 paragraph).
2. Mechanism interpretation — what the results mean in physical terms.
3. Quantitative comparison with prior work — explain reasons for agreement or disagreement.
4. Implications for the broader field, other regions, or future work.
5. Limitations — specific, honest assessment of assumptions, uncertainties, and boundary conditions.
6. Forward look — one specific open question or next step.

### Caveat handling [OBSERVED]
- JGR-Oceans expects honest assessment of limitations. A dedicated limitations paragraph or subsection is acceptable and common.
- Three patterns observed:
  1. Single limitations paragraph at end of Discussion. [Fu 2023, Cai 2024]
  2. Multiple caveat paragraphs distributed through Discussion. [Li 2022]
  3. Limitations embedded in individual argument paragraphs. [Martínez-Moreno 2022]
- All are acceptable. The key is: limitations must be specific, not boilerplate.

### Must do
- Restate main findings to anchor the discussion.
- Provide quantitative comparison with prior work.
- Discuss which assumptions matter most and what would change if they were violated.
- End with a forward-looking statement.

### Avoid
- Restating Results in detail without interpretation.
- Generic limitation statements ("more research is needed").
- Expanding into unsupported speculation.
- New data, new mechanism, or claims absent from Results.

## Methods

### Section role
Sufficient detail for reproducibility by a specialist. Methods BEFORE Results (IMRaD). [OBSERVED 5/5]

### Required detail
- Data sources: access information, spatial/temporal coverage, resolution, product version. [OBSERVED]
- Processing steps that affect the central claim, with justification for choices made.
- Statistical methods with justification (why this test, why this threshold).
- Model configuration: resolution, forcing, parameterizations, experiment design.
- Algorithm descriptions sufficient for reproduction.
- Budget calculations or diagnostic methods with equations.

### Must do
- Organize in subsections by data type or analytical step. [OBSERVED]
- Study area description can be a separate subsection or integrated into data description.
- JGR-Oceans Methods are longer and more detailed than GRL Methods.
- Validation of methods against independent data belongs in main text for the central claim.

### Avoid
- "Data were processed using standard methods" without specifying parameters.
- Placing primary validation of the central method only in supplementary material.
- Missing key methodological information needed for reproducibility.

## Conclusion

### Section role
Summarize the complete story. JGR-Oceans typically merges Conclusion with Discussion. [OBSERVED 4/5 merge]

### Must do
- Restate the problem, approach, and key findings succinctly.
- State broader significance — scientific first, societal second.
- Optionally include 1–2 forward-looking sentences.

### Format options
- Merged "Discussion and Conclusions" or "Conclusions and Discussion" — both standard. [OBSERVED]
- Numbered key-point list acceptable within merged section. [OBSERVED: Cai 2024]
- Standalone "Conclusion" section possible but less common. [OBSERVED: Wang 2023]

### Avoid
- Introducing new evidence or analysis.
- Repeating methodological details.
- Policy recommendations without supporting evidence.

## Abstract

### Section role
Communicate the full scope of the study. Self-contained, accessible to a broad ocean science audience. [OBSERVED]

### Architecture [OBSERVED]
1. Problem context (1 sentence).
2. Knowledge gap (1 sentence).
3. Approach and data (1–2 sentences).
4. Key results with quantification (1–2 sentences).
5. Implication or significance (1 sentence).

### Must do
- Start with the phenomenon's importance, not generic background.
- Include key quantitative results.
- End with specific implication — not generic filler.

### Avoid
- Method-first opening (less common in JGR than in GRL).
- Excessive jargon and acronyms.
- Overly long — JGR has abstract length limits.

## Title and Keywords

### Title format [OBSERVED 5/5]
- Title Case (all major words capitalized).
- Noun phrase, never a declarative sentence.
- Pattern: "[Process/phenomenon] [preposition/participle] [context/region]"
- "Characteristics and Dynamics of the Interannual Eddy Kinetic Energy Variation in the Central Pacific Sector of the Southern Ocean"
- "Global Mapping of Mesoscale Eddy Vertical Tilt"
- "Climatology, Seasonality, and Trends of Spatially Coherent Ocean Eddies"

### Must do
- Include the key variable, process, AND study region in the title.
- Descriptive and complete — JGR titles are often longer than GRL titles.
- Keep under ~25 words.

### Avoid
- Declarative-sentence titles.
- Question titles.
- Colon titles (less common than in some journals, though used occasionally).

### Keywords
- JGR-Oceans publishes a keyword block with the article. [OBSERVED]
- Select 5–8 keywords that define the paper's subfield for indexing.
- Include: key process, methodology, geographic region, data type.

## Figures, Tables, and Captions

### Figure logic
- One scientific question per main figure.
- Each figure supports one aspect of the evidence chain.
- JGR-Oceans allows more figures than GRL (typically 6–12 main-text figures).

### Caption style [OBSERVED]
- Self-contained: a reader should understand the figure without referring to the main text.
- First sentence states what is shown.
- Remaining sentences describe panels, data sources, methods, sample sizes, statistics.
- Panel labels: lowercase (a, b, c) in bold.
- Always "Figure" (not "Fig.").

### Must do
- Include time period, sample size, and statistical test in every caption.
- Define all abbreviations in each caption (self-contained).

### Avoid
- Panel inventory without a scientific question.
- Captions that assume the reader has already read the main text.

## Data, Code, and Supplementary Materials

### Data availability [OBSERVED]
- "Data Availability Statement" section after Conclusions.
- List every dataset with: full product name, version, persistent DOI/URL.
- AGU requires data used in figures to be archived in a public repository.

### Code availability
- State software/version for each key analysis step.
- Provide repository DOI or URL for custom analysis code.

### Supplementary materials
- Detailed tuning parameters, extended validation, derivations of standard equations → Supporting Information.
- Individual case studies supporting but not essential to the central argument → SI.
- JGR-Oceans has no strict page limit for SI.
- Supplementary figures follow the same caption rules as main figures.

### Avoid
- Placing essential evidence for the central claim only in Supplementary.
- Missing data availability statement (AGU requirement at submission).

## Late-stage Polish

### Compression priorities
Preserve during shortening:
1. The complete evidence chain — each link from data through analysis to interpretation.
2. Validation results establishing credibility of the central claim.
3. The mechanism interpretation distinguishing the study from purely descriptive work.
4. Uncertainty assessment and limitation acknowledgment.

Cut first:
1. Extended literature review in Introduction.
2. Individual case studies supporting but not advancing the central argument.
3. Secondary validation against datasets less relevant to the central claim.
4. Excessive methodological detail → Supplementary.
5. Speculative implications without supporting evidence.

### Voice alignment
- Verify claim language matches evidence strength (see Shared calibration table).
- Verify every paragraph opens with its key point (claim-first preferred but not mandatory).
- Check that limitations are specific and honest — not boilerplate.
- JGR-Oceans voice: thorough, rigorous, complete. Not fast or flashy.

### Submission-facing checks
- Verify current JGR-Oceans word limit, figure count, abstract length against the manuscript.
- Confirm Data Availability Statement, author contributions (CRediT format), and Plain Language Summary are present.
- Apply length constraints only during this pass, not during drafting.
