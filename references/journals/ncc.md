# Nature Climate Change

*Nature Climate Change*

---

## 1. Journal Narrative Identity

Nature Climate Change is a **climate-change-centered, Earth-system-relevant, consequence-driven** journal.
It does not publish oceanographic findings that are merely "relevant to climate" in a generic sense.
It publishes research where the ocean finding reveals how the climate system is changing, why it is changing, or what the consequences of that change are for the Earth system.

In ocean science, Nature Climate Change is the right home when you have:

- A finding that constitutes **detection of a climate-change signal** in the ocean — a trend, a regime shift, a response to anthropogenic forcing that can be observed and quantified
- A finding that provides **attribution of an observed ocean change** to climate forcing (anthropogenic or natural), with explicit causal chain evidence
- A finding that demonstrates a **climate feedback or Earth-system consequence** of an ocean process — how an ocean change feeds back on the climate system (heat uptake, carbon cycle, sea-level, cryosphere interaction, biogeochemical cycle)
- A finding where the **climate significance is quantified**, not merely asserted — the magnitude, the spatial scale, the temporal scale, and the mechanism of the climate connection are all part of the evidence
- A finding whose **primary significance is at the Earth-system level**, not at the oceanographic subfield level

Nature Climate Change is **not** the right home when:

- The ocean process is important for climate but there is no evidence that it is changing or responding to climate forcing — this belongs in Nature Communications or GRL as a "process with climate relevance"
- The climate connection is asserted in the title and abstract but the evidence does not extend beyond a regional oceanographic description — the climate relevance remains underdeveloped
- The study describes a climate-change impact on ocean physics but does not connect it to an Earth-system consequence (heat, carbon, sea-level, ecosystems, cryosphere) — the finding is incomplete for NCC
- The finding is purely methodological or descriptive, even if climate-relevant — a new algorithm for detecting ocean trends belongs in JGR or ESSD;
what the algorithm reveals about climate change belongs in NCC
- The paper documents a regional ocean change but cannot demonstrate or rigorously argue its broader climate significance — regional description without climate-system framing belongs in JGR-Oceans

The core narrative personality of Nature Climate Change is: **climate-first, evidence-driven, consequence-focused**.
A successful NCC ocean science paper answers three questions: (1) What is changing in the ocean that matters for climate? (2) What is causing this change? (3) What does this change mean for the Earth system?
The climate problem is the protagonist, not the ocean process — the ocean process is the vehicle through which a climate-relevant finding is revealed.

## 2. Best-fit Ocean Science Stories

### Detection-attribution story

An observed ocean change (trend, regime shift, spatial reorganization) that can be attributed to anthropogenic forcing or natural climate variability with quantified confidence.
The detection must be robust (multiple datasets, sufficient record length, appropriate statistical methods), and the attribution must have explicit causal-chain evidence — not just "consistent with warming."

Exemplar NCC ocean papers often connect a detected ocean-process change to climate-relevant consequences such as heat transport, carbon uptake, ecosystem response, or model limitations — with the connection chain made explicit and evidence-bounded.

### Climate-consequence story

An ocean change (detected or projected) with quantified consequences for the Earth system: heat uptake, carbon uptake, sea-level rise, cryosphere stability, biogeochemical cycles, or marine ecosystem function.
The consequence must be quantified, not merely proposed.

### Earth-system feedback story

A mechanism by which an ocean process amplifies or dampens climate change.
For example, eddy-mediated heat uptake that accelerates under warming, or stratification-driven carbon uptake reduction.
The feedback pathway must be demonstrated with evidence, not hypothesized.

### Global-change signal with multi-dataset convergence

A finding where multiple independent observational datasets (satellite, in situ, reanalysis, proxy) converge on the same climate-change signal in the ocean.
Convergence across independent data sources is the gold standard for NCC, as the journal's broad audience needs confidence that the signal is real and not an artifact of any single data type.

### Projection-to-impact story

A modeling study that projects future ocean changes and demonstrates their Earth-system or societal consequences.
The projection must be grounded in a realistic model, the uncertainty must be quantified, and the impact must be specifically connected to the projected change.

### Stories that belong elsewhere:

| Better fit for | Story characteristics |
|---------------|----------------------|
| Nature Communications | Broad significance, cross-disciplinary, but the climate connection is potential rather than demonstrated; the finding is about a fundamental process rather than a climate-change signal |
| GRL | Single sharp finding about an ocean process; climate relevance stated but not the central narrative |
| JGR-Oceans | Complete regional description, multi-evidence oceanographic study without climate consequence quantification |
| JPO | Dynamical mechanism of an ocean process; climate relevance is secondary context |
| Nature | Paradigm-shifting climate finding with broadest scientific significance; requires exceptional evidence |

## 3. Preferred Reasoning and Evidence Logic

### Claim structure

NCC manuscripts typically establish a climate-relevant pathway that includes one or more of these elements:

| Pathway element | What supports it | What it achieves |
|----------------|-----------------|------------------|
| Detection | Observations, statistics, trend analysis, multi-dataset consistency | Demonstrates that the change is real, significant, and not an artifact of data or methodology |
| Attribution | Causal-chain evidence, forcing-response analysis, model experiments, physical reasoning | Connects the observed change to a climate driver (anthropogenic or natural) with explicit mechanism |
| Consequence | Quantified impact on heat, carbon, sea-level, ecosystems, cryosphere, or climate feedback | Shows why this change matters at the Earth-system level, not just within oceanography |

Papers may emphasize one or two of these elements.
A paper with robust detection and a well-argued Earth-system consequence — even without full causal attribution — can fit NCC if the connection chain is explicit and evidence-bounded.
Similarly, a mechanism-focused paper with clear projection relevance can be appropriate if the climate connection is quantified and uncertainty is addressed.
The key requirement is that the broader climate implication has an explicit evidence or reasoning chain, not that all three pathway elements are completed.

### How much evidence is enough

NCC requires **convergent evidence sufficient to convince a broad climate-science audience**:

- **Multi-dataset convergence is strongly preferred**, especially for detection claims.
A trend detected in one dataset is suggestive; the same trend confirmed in an independent dataset (different sensor, different processing, different physical principle) is convincing.
A single data source can support an NCC story when the signal is exceptionally well validated, methodologically robust, and clearly linked to a climate-relevant question.
- **Independent support strengthens the claim.** Multi-dataset convergence is not a rigid requirement, but papers with only one line of evidence should clearly address potential data artifacts and demonstrate methodological rigor.
- **Statistical rigor is expected.** Trend significance must account for autocorrelation (modified Mann-Kendall or equivalent).
Uncertainty must be quantified (confidence intervals, standard errors with effective sample size).
The effect of interannual variability (ENSO, SAM, PDO) on trend estimates must be addressed.
- **The null hypothesis must be explicitly considered.** Could the observed change be explained by natural variability, data artifacts, or methodological choices?
The paper should address these explicitly.
- **Evidence boundaries must be stated.** A bounded claim with acknowledged uncertainty is stronger than an inflated one, and makes the evidence chain visible to climate-science readers.

### Mechanism language

| Evidence base | Language |
|---------------|----------|
| Direct detection from multiple datasets with statistical significance | "We show that", "Our results demonstrate that", "We find a significant [trend/change] in" |
| Attribution supported by causal-chain evidence | "This change is attributable to", "We attribute this to", "The observed trend is consistent with forcing from" |
| Attribution plausible but not fully isolated | "This is consistent with", "Our results suggest that", "The pattern indicates that" |
| Climate consequence quantified from observations or models | "This implies a [quantified] impact on", "These changes have implications for" |
| Climate consequence plausible but not directly quantified | "May have implications for", "Could influence", "Highlights the need to assess" |

**Critical note:** NCC readers include climate scientists, not only oceanographers.
Claims about "climate feedbacks" or "carbon cycle implications" must be supported by the oceanographic evidence presented, not by citing generic climate importance.
If the paper claims a climate feedback, the feedback pathway must be demonstrated or at minimum argued with quantitative context.

### Ocean science-specific evidence logic for NCC

- **Detection strengthened by multi-dataset consistency.** An ocean trend claim is stronger when validated against an independent product (different sensor, different processing, different physical principle).
Single-dataset detection claims can still fit if the data quality, validation, and methodological choices are well documented.

- **Record length and trend separation.** NCC trend papers must demonstrate that the observational record is long enough to separate the climate-change signal from natural variability (ENSO, PDO, SAM, decadal modes).
Methods for isolating the forced signal (linear trend, low-pass filtering, signal-to-noise analysis, and comparison with control runs or fingerprint patterns) should be documented and justified.

- **Regional-to-global connection.** A regional ocean change must be connected to a broader climate process or consequence.
A paper showing that the Gulf Stream is shifting poleward is a regional oceanographic finding; showing that this shift alters basin-scale heat transport or carbon uptake is an NCC finding.

- **Attribution depth guides claim language.** NCC expects more than "this change is consistent with global warming." When causal evidence is available (wind stress change, stratification change, buoyancy forcing, model experiments), stronger attribution language is justified.
When only consistent patterns exist, bounded language such as "consistent with" or "suggests" is appropriate.

- **Uncertainty as narrative element.** NCC papers should not hide uncertainty but should use it to bound the claim.
"The observed trend of X ± Y is significant at the 95% level, but the record length does not allow separation of the anthropogenic signal from decadal variability" is an honest bounded claim that strengthens credibility.

### Uncertainty handling

- Quantitative uncertainty is expected for all central claims (confidence intervals, standard errors, ensemble spread).
- Sources of uncertainty must be identified:
observational uncertainty (measurement error, sampling, processing choices), statistical uncertainty (autocorrelation, record length), and dynamical uncertainty (model spread, parameter sensitivity).
- When uncertainty prevents a definitive claim, state what additional evidence would resolve it.
This is more constructive than generic "further research needed" language.
- Do not hide large uncertainty behind dense hedging.
A single clear bounded statement is more credible than a paragraph of "may possibly potentially."

## 4. Section-level Story Strategy

### Introduction

NCC Introduction must **open with the climate problem, not the oceanographic problem**.
The first paragraph should establish why the climate-system question matters — heat uptake, carbon cycle, sea-level rise, cryosphere stability, biogeochemical cycles, climate feedbacks.
The oceanographic context is introduced in the second paragraph, framed as the vehicle through which a climate-relevant finding will be revealed.

Strong Introduction arc:
- **Paragraph 1:** Open with the climate problem.
What Earth-system process is poorly understood, changing, or consequential?
Why does this matter for the climate?
The opening should be accessible to climate scientists across subfields.
- **Paragraph 2:** Narrow to the specific ocean process.
What role does this ocean process play in the climate problem?
What is known, and what is the critical knowledge gap that prevents understanding of the climate-system response?
- **Paragraph 3:** State the contribution.
What does this study do?
What data, approach, and analysis?
What is the central finding?
What is the climate significance?
The key finding and its climate implication should both be stated here.

Avoid: opening with a regional oceanographic description ("The Kuroshio Extension is a region of high eddy kinetic energy...") — that framing is for JGR or JPO.
For NCC, the opening must be about the climate system.

### Methods (Data and Methods)

NCC Methods should be **thorough enough for evaluation by a climate scientist who is not an oceanographer**. Structure:

- **Data sources:** Access information, spatial/temporal coverage, resolution, processing version.
For satellite data: the specific product, temporal coverage, spatial resolution, and key processing choices.
- **Statistical methods:** How trends were computed (method, significance test, autocorrelation treatment).
How uncertainty was quantified.
How interannual variability was separated from the trend.
- **Key analytical steps:** Decomposition methods, filtering choices, threshold definitions.
Justification for methodological choices that affect the central claim.
- **Attribution methods (if applicable):** Model experiments, forcing-response analysis, fingerprinting approach.

Methods can appear after the main text (Nature-series format) or as a separate section.
Detailed sensitivity tests, extended validation, and additional case studies belong in supplementary material.

### Results

NCC Results should be organized around the **narrative arc of detection → attribution → consequence**, not around individual figures.
A typical structure:

- **Establish the detection** early in the Results.
Show the observed change, its statistical significance, its spatial pattern, and its robustness across multiple datasets.
Lead with the finding, then present the evidence.
- **Address confounding factors** (end of detection section).
Show that the signal is not an artifact of interannual variability, data processing, or record length.
This strengthens the detection claim.
- **Present the attribution.** Connect the detected change to a climate driver with evidence.
This can be observational co-variability, physical reasoning, or model experiments.
- **Demonstrate the consequence** in the final results subsection.
Show what the change means for the climate system: heat flux, carbon uptake, sea-level, ecosystems.
This is the "so what" of the NCC story.

Each figure should serve one narrative purpose. Self-contained captions. Quantitative results with uncertainties.

### Discussion

NCC Discussion should **elevate the finding to its broader climate significance**:

1. **Restate the core finding** briefly in climate-system context.
2. **Compare with prior work** — especially where the new result changes or refines previous understanding.
Quantitative comparison is expected.
3. **Discuss attribution depth** — how confident is the attribution?
What drives the observed change, and what remains uncertain?
4. **Articulate the climate-system implication** — this is where the paper answers "why does this matter for climate?" Be specific:
quantified impacts, feedback pathways, model representation gaps.
5. **Bound the claim** — what cannot be concluded?
What record length, data coverage, or methodology limitations affect the conclusion?
6. **Forward look** — one specific question this finding opens, or one prediction it enables.
Not generic "more research needed."

NCC Discussion should not repeat Results in detail, expand into unsupported climate speculation, or end with a generic limitations paragraph.

### Conclusion

NCC Conclusion should be **brief and climate-focused**: restate the observed change, its attribution (what is known), and its Earth-system significance (why it matters).
One paragraph, no new evidence.

### Abstract

NCC Abstract should tell a concise climate story:
climate problem → what was done → what was found (observed change with key quantification) → climate implication.

Start with the climate problem, not the ocean process.
End with the Earth-system significance.
The abstract must make a climate scientist in any subfield understand why the finding matters.
A typical NCC ocean science abstract structure:

> [Climate problem sentence.] [Knowledge gap sentence.] [Approach and data sentence.] [Key finding with quantification.] [Climate implication sentence.]

References in abstracts: use sparingly or not at all.

## 5. Writing Voice and Rhetorical Style

### Sentence and paragraph rhythm

NCC writing is **clear, direct, and accessible to the full climate science community** (atmospheric scientists, cryospheric scientists, biogeochemists, policy researchers).
Sentences are varied but never dense.
Paragraphs are compact and each advances the narrative.

- Active voice throughout:
"We analyze 27 years of satellite data" not "An analysis of 27 years of satellite data was conducted."
- Lead paragraphs with the key point, then support with evidence.
- Avoid filler transitions:
"Importantly", "Notably", "Interestingly" — cut these unless they signal genuine significance.
- Use short, declarative sentences for central claims:
"The global surface area-integrated EKE has a positive trend of ~1.2% per decade." Longer, nuanced sentences for context and caveats.

### Claim strength and hedging

NCC claims must be **confident for the detection, careful for the attribution, and quantified for the consequence**:

| Claim type | Appropriate language |
|-----------|-------------------|
| Robust detection (multi-dataset, significant) | "We show that", "Our results demonstrate that", "We find a significant [trend] of [X] per decade" |
| Detection with caveats | "Our analysis reveals", "The data indicate", "We find evidence of" |
| Attribution with evidence | "This change is attributable to", "The observed trend is consistent with forcing from" |
| Attribution suggestive | "This suggests", "This is consistent with", "The pattern indicates" |
| Quantified consequence | "This implies a [X%] change in", "This corresponds to [Y] of additional heat/carbon" |
| Consequence plausible | "May have implications for", "Could influence", "Highlights the need to" |

Do not inflate detection claims beyond what the data support.
If the record is too short to separate the trend from decadal variability, say so.
Do not inflate attribution claims beyond what the causal evidence supports.

### Level of technicality

NCC is read by a **broad climate science audience — not just oceanographers**.
This is the most critical writing constraint for ocean science submissions:

- Define all oceanographic terms on first use, including terms that are standard across physical oceanography.
"Eddy kinetic energy (EKE)" with a brief explanation.
"Mixed-layer depth (MLD)" with context.
- Minimize acronyms. Define all on first use. Consider whether a term needs an acronym at all for a short NCC article.
- Mathematical formulations should be in Methods, not the main text.
Key results can reference equations by number but should state the physical meaning in natural language.
- Quantitative results should be framed for interpretability:
"an increase of ~1.2% per decade" is accessible;
raw values with dense units should be accompanied by context ("...equivalent to an energy input of the same order as the global internal tide energy budget").
- **The most common NCC writing mistake by oceanographers:** assuming the reader knows what EKE, MLD, SST gradient magnitude, or baroclinic instability mean without explanation.
Define them.
Make the physical meaning clear.

### Mechanism language — special note for ocean science in NCC

NCC ocean science papers often involve mechanism interpretation to connect detection to attribution.
The language must match the evidence:

- **When you have direct attribution evidence** (wind stress trends driving EKE increase via diagnosed baroclinic instability changes):
stronger attribution language is appropriate:
"The observed EKE increase is attributable to strengthening wind stress forcing."
- **When you have consistent patterns but no direct forcing-response diagnostic:** "This pattern is consistent with", "These results suggest that wind forcing plays a role in" — plausible mechanism language, bounded.
- **When the mechanism is inferred but not diagnosed:** "may reflect", "could result from" — clearly bounded speculation.

For the **consequence** portion of the narrative, the language should be quantitative where possible:
"The observed EKE increase implies an additional ~1.0 × 10¹⁷ J of mesoscale energy, comparable to..." rather than "this may have significant implications for ocean heat content."

### Broader significance framing

Broader significance in NCC must be **climate-system significance, framed in Earth-system terms**.
The significance is not that "this tells us something new about ocean dynamics" (that is JPO significance) or "this provides a complete description of a regional process" (that is JGR significance).
The significance is: "this changes our understanding of how the climate system is responding to forcing."

**Good NCC significance framing** connects a specific ocean change to an Earth-system consequence with quantitative context and a clear evidence boundary.

**Not good (for NCC):** "These results provide new insights into eddy dynamics in the Southern Ocean." — This is too narrow for NCC.
The significance must reach the climate-system level.

**Not good (for NCC):** "These results have important implications for climate policy." — The implication chain must be scientific first;
policy claims require evidence not present in most ocean science papers.

## 6. Late-stage Compression and Submission Notes

### What to preserve during compression

- The detection evidence (multi-dataset convergence, statistical significance)
- The attribution or causal-chain evidence
- The climate-system consequence quantification
- The figures that establish the detection → attribution → consequence narrative

### What to cut first if over length

- Extended regional context or literature review in Introduction
- Detailed methodological processing steps (move to supplementary)
- Secondary figures showing individual case studies or regional details
- Extended discussion of mechanisms that are not directly related to the climate consequence

### Hard constraints to verify

At the final pre-submission stage, check the journal's current author guidelines for word count, figure count, abstract length, and format requirements.
Apply these only during the late polishing pass, not during drafting.
Verify data availability statement, author contributions, and competing interests declarations.

### Structure fit notes

- Check that the title, abstract, and opening paragraph foreground a climate-change or Earth-system problem, not only an oceanographic process.
- Check that any climate implication has an explicit evidence or reasoning chain — attribution language should be bounded by the available evidence.
- Check that ocean-specific terms (EKE, MLD, stratification, baroclinic instability) are defined for climate-science readers who may not be oceanographers.
- Check that the broader significance is quantified or clearly connected to an Earth-system consequence, rather than stated in generic terms.
