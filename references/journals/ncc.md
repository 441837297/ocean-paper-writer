## Journal Identity

### Core narrative identity
- Climate-first, evidence-driven, consequence-focused.
- The ocean process is the vehicle; the climate-system finding is the protagonist.
- Every NCC ocean paper must answer three questions:
  1. What is changing in the ocean that matters for climate?
  2. What is causing this change?
  3. What does this change mean for the Earth system?

### Best-fit stories
- **Detection-attribution**: observed ocean change attributed to forcing with explicit causal-chain evidence.
- **Climate-consequence**: ocean change with quantified Earth-system impact.
- **Earth-system feedback**: mechanism by which an ocean process amplifies or dampens climate change.
- **Multi-dataset convergence**: same signal confirmed across independent observational platforms.
- **Projection-to-impact**: modeled future ocean changes with quantified consequences.

### Not a good fit when
- Climate connection is asserted but evidence does not extend beyond regional oceanography.
- Finding is methodological or descriptive, even if climate-relevant.
- Regional ocean change without demonstrated connection to broader climate significance.
- Ocean process is important for climate but there is no evidence it is changing.

### Evidence logic
- Multi-dataset convergence strongly preferred for detection claims. [OBSERVED]
- Single-dataset detection acceptable only if exceptionally well validated and methodological choices are documented.
- Statistical rigor is expected: account for autocorrelation (modified Mann-Kendall or equivalent), quantify uncertainty, separate trend from interannual variability.
- The null hypothesis (natural variability, data artifacts) must be explicitly addressed.
- Evidence boundaries must be stated. A bounded claim with acknowledged uncertainty is stronger than an inflated one.

## Shared

### Global writing discipline
- Active voice throughout: "We analyze..." not "An analysis was conducted." [OBSERVED]
- Lead every paragraph with its key point. [OBSERVED]
- Use one term for one concept throughout the manuscript.
- Define every oceanographic term on first use with a brief plain-language explanation. [OBSERVED]
- Minimize acronyms. Do not create an acronym for a term used ≤3 times.
- Mathematical formulations → Methods. Main text states physical meaning in natural language.
- Short declarative sentences for central claims; longer sentences for context and caveats.

### Claim language calibration
| Evidence level | Use |
|---|---|
| Multi-dataset, statistically significant detection | "We show that..."; "We find a significant [trend] of [X] per decade" |
| Detection with caveats | "The data indicate..."; "We find evidence of..." |
| Attribution with causal-chain evidence | "This change is attributable to..." |
| Attribution plausible, not fully isolated | "This is consistent with..."; "These results suggest..." |
| Speculative implication | "May influence..."; "Could affect..." |
| Cross-model or cross-dataset agreement | Use "robust" only when agreement is demonstrated |

### Figure references
- Always parenthetical after the claim: "(Fig. 2a)". [OBSERVED 4/4]
- Never use "Figure X shows..." or "As shown in Fig. X..." as a main clause. [OBSERVED 4/4]
- Panel letters: lowercase. Supplementary: "Extended Data Fig. X", "Supplementary Fig. X". [OBSERVED]

### Forbidden patterns (all sections)
| Do not write | Write instead |
|---|---|
| "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Importantly" | Let the evidence carry the emphasis |
| "It is worth noting that..." | State the point directly |
| "In order to..." | "To..." |
| Rhetorical questions | Declarative transition |
| "This paper is organized as follows..." | Nothing — proceed to the next paragraph |
| "It is important to clarify that..." | State the clarification directly |
| "Taken together..." after every paragraph | "Together, these results indicate..." only at subsection close |
| "[claim], which has important implications for climate." | Specify the implication with quantification or a concrete pathway |

## Introduction

### Section role
Establish the climate significance of the ocean phenomenon, identify the specific knowledge gap, and state the paper's contribution.

### Architecture (3–5 paragraphs) [OBSERVED 5/6]
1. **P1 — Broad context**: Introduce the ocean phenomenon and connect it to climate significance within the first two sentences.
2. **P2 — Narrow to the gap**: What is known? What specific obstacle prevents understanding?
3. **P3 — This paper's contribution**: "Here, using [approach], we [finding]."

### Opening move [OBSERVED 3/4]
- Open with the ocean phenomenon, but frame it through its climate-system role immediately.
- OK: "Subtropical western boundary currents (WBCs)… serve as efficient conveyors of mass and energy between tropical and extratropical regions."
- OK: "Ocean fronts are boundaries that separate distinct water masses. … They are thought to play a crucial role in air–sea CO2 fluxes…"
- Avoid: "The Kuroshio Extension is a region of high eddy kinetic energy…" without immediate climate connection.

### Gap statement template
"However, owing to [specific obstacle], [specific unknown] remains unknown. Here, using [approach], we find [result in one sentence]."
- "More research is needed" or "this question remains open" is not a gap statement.

### Must do
- Include the key quantified finding in the final paragraph.
- Keep the contribution statement to 1–2 sentences of approach + finding.

### Avoid
- Full mechanism preview (save for Results).
- "This paper is organized as follows…"
- Opening with a narrow regional/oceanographic description that lacks climate framing.
- Definition of variables that belong in Results or Methods.

## Results

### Section role
Present the evidence chain: detection → robustness → attribution → consequence. Each subsection advances one link.

### Evidence ladder [OBSERVED]
1. **System setup**: define the variable, dataset, time period, and diagnostic frame.
2. **Detection**: show the change, its magnitude, significance, and spatial pattern.
3. **Robustness**: rule out artifacts from data processing, record length, interannual variability.
4. **Attribution**: connect the change to a climate driver (when causal evidence allows).
5. **Consequence**: quantify what the change means for the Earth system.
6. **Boundary**: state what cannot be concluded from the available evidence.

### Paragraph opening rule [OBSERVED]
- Default: open with the finding (claim-first).
- Exception: the first paragraph of a new subsection may open with method or context when introducing a new diagnostic. Return to claim-first in the next paragraph.
- The closing paragraph of a subsection may synthesize across preceding findings.

### Paragraph structure
Claim → quantified evidence → spatial/temporal/uncertainty detail → interpretation or boundary.
Keep to 3–5 sentences. Never end a paragraph with only a figure reference.

### Results headings [OBSERVED]
- Sentence case throughout.
- Noun phrases for descriptive subsections: "Identification of key frontal areas."
- Declarative sentences for central findings: "Eddy-rich regions become richer."
- Never: Title Case, variable labels as headings, or "we" headings.

### Avoid
- "To examine whether…" as routine paragraph opener.
- Ending paragraphs with "(Fig. 2a)." — close with interpretation.
- Method-first openings beyond the subsection-start exception.
- Variable labels or method names as headings.

## Discussion

### Section role
Elevate the finding to its broader climate significance. Reinterpret, contextualize, extend — do NOT repeat Results. NCC papers often merge Discussion with a brief conclusion ("Summary and discussion"). [OBSERVED 3/4]

### Architecture [OBSERVED]
1. Core finding restated in climate-system context (1 paragraph).
2. Comparison with prior work — quantitative where possible.
3. Mechanism interpretation — bounded by evidence.
4. Climate-system implication — specific and quantified.
5. Caveats — embedded in argument flow, never a standalone "Limitations" paragraph.
6. Forward look — one specific question or prediction.

### Caveat sandwich [OBSERVED]
"Although [limitation], [evidence the finding holds]. Nevertheless, [finding stands]."
Example: "Although sampling uncertainty remains largest in the Southern Ocean, the same intensification appears across independent products."

### Must do
- First sentence: restate the central finding without repeating figure-by-figure Results.
- Embed every caveat: state the limitation, then why the finding still stands.
- Close with one specific implication or next question.

### Avoid
- Summarizing every Results subsection in order.
- Standalone "Limitations" paragraph at the end.
- Expanding into unsupported climate speculation.
- Generic closing: "more research is needed" or "these results have important implications."
- New data, new mechanism, or claims absent from Results.

## Methods

### Section role
Provide enough detail for a climate scientist who is not an oceanographer to evaluate the evidence. Nature format: after the main text. [OBSERVED 4/4]

### Required detail
- Data sources: access information, spatial/temporal coverage, resolution, product version. [OBSERVED]
- Processing choices that affect the central claim, with justification.
- Statistical methods: trend estimation method, significance test, autocorrelation treatment.
- How interannual variability was separated from the trend signal.
- Uncertainty quantification: all sources identified and estimated.
- Sensitivity tests for key methodological choices.

### Must do
- State why each methodological choice was made, not only what was done.
- Report effective sample size when autocorrelation is present.
- Keep detailed processing steps, extended validation, and sensitivity tests in Supplementary.

### Avoid
- Overlong processing detail in main text → move to Supplementary.
- Missing uncertainty treatment for the central claim.
- Assuming readers know why a specific dataset or method was chosen.

## Abstract

### Section role
Tell a concise climate story: problem → approach → key quantified finding → climate implication. 5–7 sentences. [OBSERVED]

### Architecture [OBSERVED]
1. Climate problem or knowledge gap (1–2 sentences).
2. Approach and data (1 sentence).
3. Key quantified finding — magnitude, spatial scale, time period (1–2 sentences).
4. Climate-system implication (1 sentence).

### Must do
- Start with the problem or gap, not the method. [OBSERVED 4/4]
- Include the central quantification in the abstract body.
- End with Earth-system significance, not generic filler. A strongly quantified finding may serve as the closing sentence when it carries the implication implicitly. [OBSERVED 5/6]

### Avoid
- Method-first opening.
- Full mechanism chain (one causal step is enough).
- Generic final sentence.
- References (sparingly or not at all). [OBSERVED]

## Conclusion

### Section role
NCC papers do not have a standalone Conclusion section. The conclusion function is served by the final subsection of Discussion ("Summary and discussion") or the closing paragraph of the last Results subsection. [OBSERVED 4/4]

### Must do
- Restate the observed change with key quantification in one sentence.
- State what is known about attribution in one sentence.
- State the Earth-system significance in one sentence.
- Keep to one paragraph. No new evidence.

### Avoid
- Separate "Conclusion" heading — use "Summary and discussion" instead.
- New data, new mechanism, or unsupported extrapolation.
- Policy recommendations without supporting evidence.

## Title and Keywords

### Title format [OBSERVED 4/4]
- Noun phrase, never a declarative sentence.
- Pattern: "[Ocean process] [preposition/participle] [climate context/consequence]"
- "Onshore intensification of subtropical western boundary currents in a warming climate"
- "Global changes in oceanic mesoscale currents over the satellite altimetry record"
- "High-latitude Southern Ocean warming hotspot induced by ocean mesoscale eddies"

### Must do
- Include both the key ocean variable AND the climate connection in the title.
- Place the ocean phenomenon as the head noun; append climate context as modifier.
- Keep under ~20 words.

### Avoid
- Declarative-sentence titles: "Ocean mesoscale eddies are intensifying under climate change."
- "X: Y" colon titles. [OBSERVED 0/4]
- Question titles.
- Foregrounding "climate change" as the head noun when the paper's contribution is about an ocean process.

### Keywords
- NCC does not publish a keyword block in the final article. [OBSERVED 4/4]
- [INFERRED] Ensure the title and abstract contain the 3–5 terms that define the paper's subfield for indexing.

## Figures, Tables, and Captions

### Figure logic
- One scientific question per main figure.
- Each figure advances one link in the detection → attribution → consequence chain.
- [INFERRED] NCC main-text figures: typically 4–5. Check current journal limits at submission.

### Caption style [OBSERVED]
- Self-contained: a reader should understand the figure without referring to the main text.
- First sentence states the finding shown (declarative). Do not start with "Figure X shows…"
- Remaining sentences describe panels, data sources, methods, sample sizes, and statistics.
- Panel labels: lowercase bold (a, b, c).

### Must do
- Include time period, sample size, and statistical test in every caption.
- Define all abbreviations in each caption (self-contained).

### Avoid
- Panel inventory without a scientific question.
- Captions that assume the reader has already read the main text.
- Starting the caption with "Figure X shows…" — state the finding directly.

## Data, Code, and Supplementary Materials

### Data availability [OBSERVED 4/4]
- "Data availability" subsection within Methods.
- List every dataset with: full product name, version, persistent DOI/URL.
- "Source data are provided with this paper."

### Code availability [OBSERVED 3/4]
- "Code availability" subsection within Methods.
- State the software/version for each key analysis step.
- Provide repository DOI or URL for custom analysis code.

### Supplementary materials
- Detailed sensitivity tests, extended validation, additional case studies → Supplementary.
- [INFERRED] NCC has no strict page limit for Supplementary Information.
- Supplementary items follow the same caption rules as main figures.

### Avoid
- Placing critical methodological steps only in Supplementary without a one-sentence summary in main-text Methods.
- Missing data or code availability statements at submission.

## Late-stage Polish

### Compression priorities
Preserve during shortening:
1. Detection evidence (multi-dataset convergence, statistical significance).
2. Attribution or causal-chain evidence.
3. Climate-system consequence quantification.
4. Figures establishing the detection → attribution → consequence chain.

Cut first:
1. Extended regional context or literature review in Introduction.
2. Detailed methodological processing steps (→ Supplementary).
3. Secondary figures showing individual case studies or regional detail.
4. Extended discussion of mechanisms not directly tied to the climate consequence.

### Voice alignment
- Title, abstract, P1: foreground the climate-system relevance within the first two sentences.
- Verify every oceanographic term is defined for a climate-science audience.
- Verify every climate implication has an explicit evidence or reasoning chain — delete "may have implications" without a concrete pathway.
- Check claim language matches evidence strength (see Shared calibration table).

### Submission-facing checks
- Verify current journal word limit, figure count, abstract length, and reference format against the manuscript.
- Confirm data availability, code availability, author contributions, and competing interests are present.
- Apply length constraints only during this pass, not during drafting.
